What the classical opponent does

A greedy start places bits using the evidence already placed. Local search checks single-bit flips, takes the best improvement and restarts when stuck. It does not use the hidden truth.

A cost check is a small CPU operation. A quantum shot is a circuit sample. Their counts are recorded separately; equal counts would not imply equal runtime.

Why warm-start needs an extra accounting line

Warm-start begins near the first classical candidate returned by local search. It does not begin near a revealed exact optimum or hidden truth.

That classical preparation is real work. The records include its cost checks and time, so an improved quantum distribution is not credited to quantum computation alone.

2. What “one training evaluation” means here

Angles → local statevector → average full cost → next angles. COBYLA repeats that loop. An evaluation computes the expected cost of a distribution; it is not one measured shot.

For these small circuits we calculate that average exactly on a CPU. No hardware measurements are used for training. Afterwards the angles are frozen and the circuit is sampled.

The p = 1 analytic formula remains available in the original engine. This shared comparison uses local COBYLA for all three circuit families; the formula is not a trainer for arbitrary variants.

More angles make the search harder

At p = 1, standard and warm-start have two angles. Multi-angle has one per active pair gate and one per qubit mixer.

In the 12-SNP seed-1 case that is 38 angles instead of 2. A 60-evaluation cap can stop before the larger search has settled.

An iteration-limit status is not a broken circuit. It says the optimizer exhausted its allotted work before satisfying its stopping criterion.

4. How to read the comparison honestly

One hit ≠ a good distribution

“Best cost” asks whether at least one good answer appeared. “Optimal-shot rate” asks how often good answers appeared. Both matter.

Shots ≠ restarts

Local search's 100% / 87.5% rates at 8 / 12 SNPs are per restart, not per quantum shot. They are strong classical references, not direct runtime ratios.

Three seeds ≠ a universal winner

We expose between-dataset variation and convergence status. These shallow, small-budget runs do not establish a best algorithm or quantum advantage.

Simulation modes: these paired studies use ideal Aer and a seeded synthetic 12-qubit line noise model, not live IBM calibration or QPU results. Ordinary notebook panels use the selected RUN_MODE; the training follow-up remains a deliberately local experiment.

Every detail: the six steps of make_instance
  1. Hidden answer. truth = N random letters (copy A). Copy B = 1 − truth.
  2. How many reads. Enough to see each SNP about coverage times: ⌈coverage · N / average read length⌉. The demo above uses coverage 2.4, so 8 reads; the race uses 6.
  3. Each read. A random start, a random length in read_len, a random copy (A or B). Copy its letters.
  4. Mistakes. Each letter flips with probability error and is hidden (−1) with probability missing.
  5. Gaps. Optional cut points no read crosses. The blocks on each side can flip independently, and nobody can phase them. Grading skips those boundaries (section 7).
  6. Repair. If two neighbouring SNPs inside a block share no read, add a 2-SNP read for them, so every block stays connected.

Puzzle k of every size uses seed k, so every method sees exactly the same puzzles and reruns give identical numbers.

What one pair contributes to the cost

Your answer puts SNPs i, j…zizjCost added
on the same letter (00 or 11)+1Dij: every “different” vote is unhappy
on different letters (01 or 10)−1Aij: every “same” vote is unhappy

Both rows equal (A + D)/2 + (D − A)/2 · zizj. Summing over pairs gives cost = offset + Σ Jij zi zj.

Reading J at a glance

  • J < 0 (more “same” votes): the cost drops when the two SNPs get the same letter.
  • J > 0 (more “different” votes): the cost drops when they differ.
  • J = 0: no read covers both, or the votes cancel.
  • offset = cost of an average random answer; the notebook calls it c₀ (12.5 on the toy).
Every detail: votes, signs and the toy check
  • Missing letters (−1) are skipped. Every pair of letters inside one read votes once, also pairs that are not neighbours.
  • Only the upper triangle (i < j) is kept, so each pair counts once.
  • Speed-up: two 0/1 tables, one[r, i] (read r has a 1 at SNP i) and zero[r, i]. Then one.T @ one + zero.T @ zero counts every “same” vote at once and one.T @ zero + zero.T @ one every “different” vote. Float matrices use optimized BLAS multiplication; these small integer counts remain exactly representable.
  • A z-flip of every SNP leaves every zizj unchanged, which is why an answer and its mirror always have the same cost.
  • Check 9.1: on the notebook's 9 reads over 5 SNPs, offset = 12.5 and the best cost is 1, reached by 01101 and its mirror 10010, the same as the notebook.
  • Short reads only link SNPs at most 3 apart, so J is non-zero only near the diagonal: a band of width 3. The last frame of the animation marks it; section 3 uses it.

Band DP on a real 12-SNP puzzle: slide a 3-SNP window, keep 8 numbers

arcs = non-zero J (blue = keep same, rose = keep different, thicker = stronger) · boxes = best cost so far for each setting of the last 3 SNPs

Brute force with a doubling trick

Keep the energies of all answers for the first q SNPs in a list E. Adding SNP q with letter 0 adds its couplings f; letter 1 subtracts them:

E ← [ E + f , E − f ] (the list doubles)

About 2 · 2N additions and 8 · 2N bytes (134 MB at 24 SNPs). The same list is reused by the exact QAOA simulator (section 6).

Band DP time vs puzzle size

realistic reads, one run on a laptop (experiments part 7)

Linear in N: N steps × 23 states. Brute force at 2000 SNPs would need 22000 answers.

Every detail: the DP step, and what the referee does when the band is too wide
  • State. The letters of the last w SNPs (w = bandwidth(J), 3 here), so 2w = 8 states. V[s] = the lowest cost of everything to the left, given that the window is set to s.
  • One step. For the new SNP, try both letters, add its couplings to the window, then forget the oldest SNP by keeping the smaller of its two values. Work: 2w per SNP.
  • Mirror pairs. The 8 numbers come in equal pairs (s and its flip), because a mirror answer costs the same.
  • Answer. optimum = offset + min V. The DP returns the cost, which is all the referee needs.
  • This is the Viterbi / transfer-matrix idea. Its cost grows like 2band, so long-range links (long reads, Hi-C) would make it hard. That is where a quantum method could matter.
  • Referee order in the race: enumerate when N ≤ 24 (or when the numpy simulator needs the list anyway); else band DP if the band ≤ 16; else the best cost known (annealing with 200× the budget, or the truth's cost if lower). A ref column records which one was used.

The local-field trick (used by both)

h = W z, W = J + Jᵀ
flip SNP i → ΔE = −2 zi hi

hi is how strongly the other SNPs pull SNP i. Flipping i changes the cost by ΔE without recounting everything. After a flip, h is updated with one row of W.

ΔE < 0 means the flip helps.

Every detail: the exact rules of both methods

Greedy start (2N tries). Visit the SNPs in a random order. Give each the letter that agrees best with the SNPs already placed: field = Σ Wij zj over placed j; field > 0 → letter 1, field < 0 → letter 0, field = 0 → coin flip.

Local search (N tries per scan). Compute ΔE for all N single flips, apply the best one if it lowers the cost. If none does, the answer is a local minimum (“stuck”): restart from a fresh greedy answer while the budget allows.

Annealing (1 try per proposal). Start from a random answer. Each step proposes flipping one random SNP. Better or equal: accept. Worse by ΔE: accept with chance e−ΔE/T. T falls geometrically from hot (the largest total |coupling| of any SNP, so almost anything is accepted) to cold (5% of the smallest |J|, so only improvements pass).

Measuring success. Each chain records hit, the try at which it first reached the optimum (∞ if never). The fraction of 64 chains with a hit within 2048 is P_budget; section 7 turns the hits into TTS99.

The circuit (same as the notebook)

4 of N qubits shown · one qubit per SNP

What each block does

  • H on every qubit: start with all 2N answers equally likely.
  • Cost layer (angle γ): one RZZ(2γ Jij / scale) per pair with Jij ≠ 0. Each answer gets a phase proportional to its cost. scale = max(1, max |J|).
  • Mixer (angle β): RX(2β) on every qubit. It mixes answers that differ by one letter, so the phases interfere.
  • Repeat p times, then measure: each shot returns one answer. Good angles make low-cost answers likely.

Training p = 1: calculate the cost directly, then search one angle

Think of two knobs: γ controls how strongly the circuit marks costs; β controls how strongly it mixes answers. Training means choosing their settings so the average measured cost is low. The formula predicts that average, not the best haplotype itself.

The baseline: try both knobs

Choose γ and βCOBYLA proposes settings.
Simulate the stateStore all 2N amplitudes and calculate the average cost.
Adjust and repeatTry to lower that average.

The baseline also trains with zero shots: its training uses a classical statevector, not hardware measurements.

Our p = 1 method: a shortcut

Choose γTry a grid of values.
Calculate the best βA mathematical formula gives it directly, using J.
Keep the lowest costRefine γ near the best grid point.

We still optimize. We replace the full-state simulation and two-knob search with direct arithmetic and a one-knob search. This is our depth-1 shortcut, not a formula for every QAOA variant or depth.

Where it came from: Ozaeta, van Dam & McMahon derived the average cost mathematically for single-layer QAOA on Ising problems (2020 preprint). It is not a fitted guess. Our implementation specializes their result to our pair-only J model. T1(γ) and T2(γ) are intermediate numbers calculated from J: no simulated state and no 2N list, so it works at 100+ SNPs.

E(γ, β) = offset + ½ sin 4β · T₁(γ) − ½ sin² 2β · T₂(γ)
  • Best β for a fixed γ is a formula: rewrite E as a sin 4β + b cos 4β + const; its minimum is const − √(a² + b²).
  • So only γ is searched: 256 grid points on (0, π], then a 1-D refinement around the best.
  • Toy result: expected cost 5.833 with 0 shots, against 7.798 for the notebook's COBYLA (check 9.4).

Same recipe, more qubits

Standard QAOA shares its angles across gates in each layer. At depth p = 3, both a 10-qubit circuit and a 100-qubit circuit need just 6 angles: three γ values and three β values.

We transfer those six settings, not the small circuit or its answer. The larger circuit is built from the larger puzzle's J.

Why this recipe can transfer

Our small and large puzzles are made the same way: similar coverage and error rates, and short reads linking nearby SNPs. At shallow depth, many gates therefore see similar local patterns.

Useful, not guaranteed. Different link patterns, weights, depth or hardware noise can need new angles. Our four held-out 14-SNP puzzles are evidence for this family, not proof for every larger puzzle.

Do reused angles lose much? (concentration)

4 unseen 14-SNP puzzles · quality = (offset − expected cost) / (offset − optimum), 1 = always optimal

Reused angles keep about 95% of the quality of tuning on each puzzle, and cost 0 shots on the new puzzle.

Why reuse works: angles barely change between puzzles

p = 1: formula angles of 85 Kaggle puzzles (8–100 SNPs) · p = 2, 3: the transferred angles of two runs

Tight clusters: good angles concentrate (Brandão et al. 2018). Deeper layers show γ growing and |β| shrinking, like a slow anneal.

Every detail: scale, γ units, β folding, INTERP and the other trainer
  • scale. The notebook divides J by max(1, max|J|) inside the RZZ, so the formula works with the physical phase γ/scale and converts back.
  • β folding. β and β + π/2 give mirror answers with the same cost, so canonical_beta folds β into (−π/4, π/4]. This makes angles from different puzzles comparable, which the median in step 3 needs.
  • INTERP (Zhou et al. 2020) pads the p angles with zeros at both ends and linearly interpolates them onto p + 1 slots: a smooth start for the deeper circuit.
  • Angles used in the race (listed as γ₁…γp, β₁…βp): p = 2 → [0.464, 0.638, −0.593, −0.304]; p = 3 → [0.387, 0.590, 0.669, −0.639, −0.447, −0.243].
  • Why p = 3 is below p = 2 on the toy: its angles were tuned on 8–12-SNP puzzles, not on this 5-SNP one. At the tested sizes from 8 through 32 SNPs, p = 3 has the lowest median TTS99 among our budgeted methods.
  • The other trainer, cobyla, is for circuits without a formula (Carla's variants). It optimises the sampled mean cost, 256 shots per evaluation, and every shot is added to TTS99.
  • Checks: 9.3 the formula equals the simulator exactly; 9.4 the formula beats COBYLA on the toy; 9.5 transferred p = 2 beats p = 1 on an unseen 13-SNP puzzle.
6 Simulators

Running 100 qubits on a laptop

KEEP: one ideal simulator for small shared puzzles. NOT NECESSARY for the main merge / QPU demo: 100-qubit MPS runs, memory and timing sweeps, and swap-direction tuning. MPS is a classical simulation technique, not a requirement for submitting a circuit to a QPU.
MPS in plain words: instead of one enormous list describing the whole quantum state, a matrix product state stores a chain of smaller blocks, one per qubit. Links between blocks retain their quantum correlations. It is a classical way to simulate the circuit, not a quantum device.

One giant list

A full statevector stores 2N amplitudes. Every extra qubit doubles the list: about 1 GB at 26, 69 GB at 32, far beyond a laptop at 100.

A chain of small blocks

qubit 1 ↔ qubit 2 ↔ qubit 3 ↔ …

The connecting links must grow when more entanglement crosses a cut through the chain. MPS saves memory only while those links stay manageable.

Why our problem fits

A read spans only 2–4 neighbouring SNPs, so gates mostly connect nearby qubits. We also use just 1–3 QAOA layers. These conditions keep entanglement across cuts limited enough for our runs.

Long-range links or deeper circuits can make the blocks much larger and remove the saving.

  • Cost layer = one multiplication: every RZZ is diagonal, so the whole layer is ψ ← e−iγE/scale ψ, with E the brute-force list from section 3.
  • Mixer: RX(2β) on qubit q mixes each pair of amplitudes that differ only in bit q; reshape(-1, 2, 2**q) lines the pairs up.
  • Exact output: P(optimum) = sum of |ψ|² over optimal answers, with no sampling noise. Matches Qiskit's statevector (check 9.3).

27–100+ SNPs: Aer MPS, sampled

  • MPS stores a small tensor (a block of numbers) per qubit. χ, the bond dimension, measures the size of the connecting links; memory grows with χ rather than requiring the full 2N list.
  • The setting that made it fast: an RZZ between qubits i and i + 2 makes Aer swap qubits until they are neighbours. The default swap direction was very slow on our banded gates; mps_swap_right is not: 99 s → about 0.1–0.2 s at 24 SNPs.
  • Above 63 qubits Aer's device description stops, so we skip the full transpile and only unroll to Aer's native gates.

Four numbers per method, per puzzle

Why it matters: “P(optimum) is high” hides how many tries it took. TTS99 turns any method into one comparable number, and it charges QAOA for training and post-processing. Switch errors check the answer against the hidden truth, the way geneticists grade phasing.

P_budget

Found the optimum within 2048 tries?

Samplers: 1 − (1 − p)2048, p = chance one shot is optimal. Chains: share of the 64 chains that hit it.

tts99

Tries to be 99% sure?

log(0.01) / log(1 − p). Lower is better. Training shots and post-processing checks are added.

score

How good was the best answer?

(offset − best) / (offset − optimum): 1 = optimum, 0 = an average random answer.

switch_err

How far from the truth?

Places where the answer jumps between copy A and copy B, only where a read links the two SNPs.

Every detail: TTS99 for chains, fairness, and the extra columns
  • TTS99 for chains (tts99_runs). A chain stopped after b tries succeeds with s(b) = share of chains with hit ≤ b. Repeating such chains costs b · log(0.01) / log(1 − s(b)) tries. We take the best stopping point b.
  • Fairness rule for QAOA: tts99 = tts99(p, 1 + extra checks / 2048) + training shots. Post-processing that checks extra answers makes each shot more expensive.
  • Budget scope. P_budget covers 2048 final samples, not an all-in cap for shot-trained plug-ins. Their training shots are added in TTS99. Classical training time is recorded separately as train_s; zero shots is not zero computation.
  • Examples: p = 1% → 458 tries. p ≥ 99% → 1 try.
  • Linked boundaries. Boundary i (between SNP i + 1 and i + 2) counts only if some read links SNPs across it. Computed with a difference array and a running sum.
  • Also stored: truth_dist (wrong letters, up to the mirror) and n_opt (how many answers tie for the optimum, always ≥ 2).

What we can claim

Three findings, stated honestly

holds up

Up to 32 SNPs, QAOA p = 3 needs the fewest tries

With reused angles and zero training shots: 3–13× fewer tries than local search. The gap narrows as N grows (12.5× at 16 SNPs, 3.9× at 32).

falls behind

Shallow QAOA loses the optimum from 48 SNPs

With p ≤ 3 the median puzzle gets no optimal shot (local search: 9% at 48). Each layer helps, so next: deeper p, Carla's variants and Alex's post-processing.

honest limit

Short reads make the puzzle classically easy

A narrow band means an exact linear-time DP (2000 SNPs in 0.15 s). An advantage claim would need long-range links.

What this comparison does not say

  • No speed-up in time: a shot on hardware and a cost check on a CPU have very different prices.
  • No hardware performance: everything is noiseless simulation. Noise and routing are Alex's track.
  • Not state of the art: our classical methods are simple. Tuned solvers, or the exact DP, do better.
